Intro

When beginning an exploration of the dataset, it is useful to start with a broad overview. The very first question we want to answer is: how many of the patients are completely healthy, and how many already show signs of heart disease? By looking at this distribution, we set the stage for everything that follows. It gives us a sense of proportion, shows us how balanced (or imbalanced) the sample is, and helps us interpret later findings in the right context.

Class Distribution

The chart below summarizes this outcome. Just over half of all patients are free of any heart disease, forming the largest single group. The rest of the population is spread across different levels of severity. The mildest form of disease is the most common among affected individuals, while the numbers gradually decrease as the severity increases. At the far end of the spectrum, only a handful of patients fall into the most severe category.

This uneven split carries an important message: most people in the dataset are either healthy or only slightly affected, while severe cases are quite rare. That means whenever we analyze risk factors, patterns, or subgroup differences, we should always keep in mind that the dataset does not treat each stage equally. The rarity of advanced disease makes it harder to generalize strong conclusions for those patients, but at the same time, it highlights how valuable their data is within the study.

After seeing the overall balance between healthy and affected patients, the natural question is: what makes these groups different? To answer this, we look at several clinical variables and compare their distributions between healthy and diseased individuals. This allows us to identify patterns and possible risk factors that tend to separate the two groups.

Age

Age is one of the strongest and most intuitive risk factors for heart disease. In the chart, we can see that healthy individuals tend to be younger, with a median age of around 52. Patients with heart disease, on the other hand, cluster at older ages, with a median of 58. While there is still some overlap, the shift to the right for the disease group makes the age difference quite clear.

In simple terms, the older the patient, the higher the chance of heart disease — a pattern that is already visible in this dataset.

Cholesterol

Cholesterol is another well-known risk factor. Both groups show a bell-shaped distribution, but the median value for patients with disease is slightly higher. What stands out is that very high cholesterol levels appear more often among those with disease than among healthy individuals.

This suggests that elevated cholesterol is more common in patients with heart disease, although the overlap shows it is not the only factor at play.

ST depression

“Oldpeak” measures ST depression on an exercise ECG, which reflects possible ischemia. The chart shows a striking contrast: healthy patients are strongly concentrated near zero, while patients with disease have a broader spread, with a much higher median.

This makes oldpeak one of the clearest markers separating healthy from diseased groups in this dataset.

Maximum heart rate

Maximum heart rate achieved during exercise (thalach) is another important signal. Healthy individuals typically reach higher heart rates, with a median of about 161. Those with heart disease peak lower, around 142.

The ability to reach a higher heart rate during exercise seems to be a protective factor, while lower peaks are more often linked to heart disease.

Resting Blood Pressure

Resting blood pressure shows more overlap between groups compared to the other variables. Both healthy and diseased patients tend to cluster in the 120–140 range, though disease cases have a slightly higher median.

While elevated blood pressure is a known risk factor, in this dataset it does not separate the groups as sharply as age, cholesterol, or oldpeak.

Taken together, these histograms show that some variables provide a much clearer separation between healthy and diseased patients than others. Age, maximum heart rate, cholesterol, and especially ST depression (oldpeak) stand out as strong indicators, with noticeable shifts between groups. In contrast, resting blood pressure, while still relevant, shows more overlap and therefore is less discriminative on its own.

The main takeaway is that heart disease does not depend on a single factor. Instead, it emerges from the combined influence of multiple clinical measurements. These first visual comparisons give us a sense of which features are more informative, and they prepare the ground for the next stage of the analysis, where we will explore categorical variables and relationships between predictors in more detail.
