Intro

After exploring how individual variables differ between healthy and diseased patients, it is equally important to examine how the population is structured across meaningful subgroups. Age and sex are two of the most fundamental factors in medicine: they not only influence disease risk, but also help us understand how representative our dataset is, and whether there are imbalances that could affect the generalizability of conclusions.

In this section, we look at how heart disease stages are distributed by age and by sex, and we also calculate the overall rate of disease within these groups. This gives us a more concrete view of which parts of the population are most affected, and whether certain subgroups stand out as being particularly vulnerable.

Stage distribution by Age

The chart below shows the proportion of patients in each disease stage across three age brackets: younger patients (≤50), middle-aged (51–60), and older patients (>60).

Among younger patients, the majority are healthy, and only a small fraction reach more advanced stages. In the 51–60 group, the picture shifts: although healthy cases still dominate, there is a noticeable rise in mild and moderate stages of heart disease. For patients over 60, the distribution is more evenly spread, with a higher share of moderate and severe cases than in younger groups. This progression reflects the cumulative effect of risk factors over time: age remains one of the strongest drivers of disease severity.

Stage distribution by Sex

Next, we compare disease stages between men and women. Biological differences, combined with lifestyle factors, often result in different risk profiles between the sexes.

The contrast is clear: women in the dataset are predominantly healthy, and relatively few reach higher stages of disease. Men, by comparison, have a much larger share of affected individuals, spread across all levels of severity. This suggests that male patients are more vulnerable in this population, which is consistent with findings from broader epidemiological studies.

Disease Rate by Age

Beyond the stage breakdown, we can also look at the simple presence or absence of disease within each age group. This allows us to summarize the overall risk in a more intuitive way.

The numbers confirm the pattern observed earlier: disease is least common among patients under 50 (about one in three). In the 51–60 group, risk rises to around half the population. Among those older than 60, more than half are already affected. This steady rise across age brackets underlines the strong and persistent effect of aging as a determinant of heart disease risk.

Disease Rate by Sex

Similarly, we can summarize disease prevalence by sex. This chart condenses the distribution into a single percentage for each group.

Here, the difference is striking: only about a quarter of women in the dataset are affected, compared to more than half of men. This reinforces the earlier observation from the stage distribution: male patients face a higher overall burden of heart disease in this population.

Conclusion

Taken together, these subgroup analyses paint a consistent picture: heart disease risk increases significantly with age and is substantially higher among men than women. The progression across age brackets highlights the cumulative impact of risk factors, while the sex difference points to both biological and lifestyle contributors. These findings remind us that when analyzing predictors or designing interventions, subgroup differences must always be kept in mind. They not only explain variation in the data, but also guide us towards more personalized approaches to prevention and treatment.
